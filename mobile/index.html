Huang's Exit Still Rattles Chips; Microsoft Outage Adds a Second Wound to Tech

Nvidia (NVDA, $171.17, +3.0% premarket) is actually bouncing after Thursday's shock, but Microsoft's (MSFT, $507.67, -2.93%) global 365 outage is now the bigger drag on Technology -- the sector is down 0.28%, the only loser among the 11 S&P sectors, while Communication and Health Care inch the index up 0.16%.

Top Story Microsoft Outage Compounds a Rough Week for Big Tech MSFT alone is 5.9% of the S&P and its -2.89% move is cutting -0.171 points off the index -- enough on its own to make Technology (-0.28%) the day's only losing sector.
Also Moving Nvidia Steadies, But the AI Trade's Nerves Haven't Why Jensen Huang's resignation Thursday after personal-conduct revelations wiped roughly $490 billion off Nvidia's market cap, per Yahoo Finance, and this morning brings fallout on two more fronts: Commerce Secretary Lutnick reportedly agreeing to testify on China chip sales tied to the scandal (CNBC), and Morningstar cutting its Nvidia fair-value estimate 19% on CEO-transition uncertainty. NVDA is bouncing +2.83% this morning and contributing +0.215 points -- the single largest mega-cap push in either direction today -- while Broadcom (-2.4%, -0.057 pts) and memory names like Micron are still sliding on fears the Nvidia vacuum invites more competition.
Worth Knowing Burry Calls a Chip Glut; AI Skeptics Get Louder Why 'Big Short' investor Michael Burry says he's vindicated as AI names like Nebius tumble over 17%, warning of a coming chip glut, per AOL.com/Moneywise -- a theme building alongside this week's broader AI-trade wobble rather than a single new headline. Not yet a clean index mover -- it's layered into the same Technology weakness as the Nvidia and Microsoft stories rather than showing up as its own line item, but it's the kind of sentiment shift worth watching if it starts showing up in sector flows next week.
Why It’s Happening

Why is Microsoft dragging on tech even as Nvidia bounces back?

The trigger
A global Microsoft 365 outage knocked out Outlook and Teams starting Thursday and is still unresolved Friday morning, with governments and airlines reporting disruptions, per CNBC, Barron's and Forbes -- and a fix that reportedly "failed to work as expected," per TipRanks.
Why it hits these stocks
An outage this broad hits Microsoft's core recurring-revenue engine (365 subscriptions) directly, raising reliability concerns right as rival Anthropic is reportedly shipping competing office tools aimed at pressuring Azure and 365 -- a competitive vulnerability, not just a one-day technical glitch.
Who’s saying what
  • CNBC Reports the outage has extended into Friday with disruptions across Outlook, Teams and other 365 services worldwide.
  • Forbes Says the outage is hitting governments and airlines, underscoring how widely embedded Microsoft's services are in critical operations.
  • shattered.io Reports Anthropic shipped four Office-rival tools, framing the timing as a direct competitive shot at Microsoft.
  • TipRanks Notes Microsoft's premarket decline accelerated after an attempted outage fix reportedly did not work as expected, suggesting the issue isn't resolved.
The other side
MSFT's move is a relatively contained 2.93% premarket decline on light trading (volume ratio 0.54, below normal) -- consistent with a name absorbing bad headlines without panic selling, not a fundamental re-rating.
What settles it
Whether Microsoft confirms a full fix and restores services before the cash open; a resolution would likely stem the slide, while a prolonged outage keeps the reliability narrative -- and the Anthropic comparison -- alive.

Apollo Global Management runs money for institutions and manages retirement-style insurance products -- think of it as part asset manager, part insurer. The business is growing fast (revenue up from $26 billion to $32 billion over the past year) but earnings have been bumpy, including a rough earnings reaction last week. The stock had fallen more than 15% from its 52-week high and dipped below its long-term trend line before today's jump. The one thing that matters most now: whether today's reassurance on private credit demand marks a real turn or just a one-day bounce in a stock that's been shaky around credit-market headlines all year.

THE BUSINESS

Apollo Is Mostly an Insurance-and-Lending Machine

Apollo's biggest line by far is Retirement Services -- essentially annuities and retirement products -- bringing in $27 billion of the firm's $32 billion in revenue and growing 23% over the past year. Asset Management, the traditional private-equity business most people associate with Apollo's name, is actually the smaller piece at $5 billion, though it's growing nearly as fast. A small Principal Investing arm (the firm's own money alongside clients') rounds it out and is growing fastest of all, at nearly 30%.

FINANCIAL HEALTH

Revenue Is Growing, But Profits Are Uneven

Total revenue rose to $32 billion last year, but the fundamentals data shows revenue actually fell 52% more recently -- a sign results here can swing sharply from one period to the next, typical for an insurer whose results move with markets. Operating margin is about 9% (9 cents of operating profit per revenue dollar), while the bottom-line profit margin is actually negative (-3%), meaning the company lost money after all costs in the most recent stretch counted. It holds about $41 billion in cash against $45 billion in debt -- a close balance, not a fortress.

HISTORY & ODDS

Past Dips Like This One Usually Recovered

In 20 past instances when Apollo traded this far below its 52-week high and below its 200-day average, the stock was higher three months later 53% of the time (average gain 4.8%), and higher a year later nearly 65% of the time, with an average 12-month gain of 27% -- though outcomes ranged widely, from a 27% loss to a 129% gain, so this is a wide spread of outcomes, not a reliable pattern.

  • Analyst consensus is "buy" with an average target roughly 15% above the current price.

  • Debt ($45 billion) roughly matches cash ($41 billion), leaving limited balance-sheet cushion if credit markets tighten further.
