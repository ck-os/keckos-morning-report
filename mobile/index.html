THIS MORNING · Premarket

Yields Keep Climbing on Fed Regime-Change Worries as Warsh Leaves Door Open to Hikes

Treasury yields are extending their march higher this morning, with the 10-year near 5.16% (+0.94%) and the 3-month/2-year/10-year complex all rising together, after reporting that Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh's overhaul of the central bank is meeting internal resistance -- and that his emerging framework leaves the door open to further rate hikes rather than cuts. Equity futures are shrugging it off for now, with S&P and Dow futures up modestly and Nasdaq futures leading at +0.67%, while gold's climb to $4,339.80 (+0.97%) alongside falling oil ($92.39, -2.35%) reads more like geopolitical hedging -- Iran's offer to reopen the Strait of Hormuz and reports of fresh Houthi attacks on Saudi targets -- than a rates-driven flight to safety. VIX has slid to 15.06, within a point of its 6-month low of 14.25, suggesting the options market isn't pricing much near-term stress from either story yet.

Warsh's push to remake the Fed is meeting internal resistance, and his emerging framework leaves the door open to more hikes -- yet equity futures haven't blinked.

The other live thread this morning is the Middle East: Iran has offered to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days and restart nuclear talks, even as the Houthis launched a fresh wave of missile and drone attacks on Saudi targets overnight -- a genuinely two-sided de-escalation/escalation story. That split is showing up cleanly in commodities: WTI crude is down 2.35% to $92.39 (de-escalation risk premium coming out), while gold is up 0.97% to $4,339.80 (safe-haven demand persisting despite the Hormuz offer). VIX, meanwhile, has eased to 15.06 -- within a point of its 6-month low of 14.25 -- suggesting options markets aren't pricing meaningful near-term stress from either the Fed or the Gulf story yet. No major US economic release lands today; the next scheduled prints are PCE and GDP on September 30, followed by the jobs report on October 2, which will be the next real test of whether Warsh's hawkish framing holds up against incoming data.
