Why It’s Happening

Why are health insurers falling while the rest of Health Care follows?

The trigger
CVS Health dropped after RBC cut its price target to $108 on separation costs, per Investing.com; Humana and UnitedHealth are also down sharply this morning, with Investor's Business Daily grouping Humana among names where 'defense' is in focus as the broader market loses ground.
Why it hits these stocks
Managed-care and pharmacy names carry heavy exposure to margin pressure from drug pricing and cost structure changes -- RBC's target cut specifically cites separation costs at CVS, and when the sector's biggest names fall together it drags the whole group lower even as the index barely moves.
Who’s saying what
  • RBC Capital Markets Cut its CVS Health price target to $108, citing separation costs, per Investing.com.
  • Seeking Alpha Argues Health Care has overtaken Tech this year, up roughly 22% year-to-date on a mix of defensive and growth characteristics -- a frame that cuts against today's weakness being a trend reversal.
  • Benzinga Lists a dozen Health Care names moving in Tuesday's premarket session, suggesting broad, name-by-name repositioning rather than one single catalyst.
The other side
Some Health Care names are bucking the slide -- HCA Healthcare (+1.59%) and Moderna (+1.26%) are both higher, and the sector's own year-to-date strength (per Seeking Alpha) argues today's drop is a pullback within a stronger multi-month trend, not a reversal.
What settles it
Wednesday's PCE and GDP releases, and any further analyst commentary on CVS's pending separation, are the near-term events that would confirm whether this is company-specific or a sector-wide repricing.

Why it’s in focus Intel fell premarket after Nvidia cut its stake in the company and SpaceX became Intel's largest holder, per Yahoo Finance, compounding Monday's 28.6% one-day crash that has already drawn shareholder lawsuits, per Tech Times and India Today.

Intel designs and makes computer chips -- the processors inside PCs, servers and data centers. The business is in the middle of a difficult turnaround: overall sales are flat to slightly down over the past three years, and the company lost money on an operating basis in 2024. The stock is down 62% from its 52-week high and just suffered its worst earnings-day drop in years. The one thing that matters most now: whether Intel's newer AI and foundry (chip-manufacturing-for-others) businesses can grow fast enough to offset a shrinking PC chip business, while the stock trades on hope more than current profit.

THE BUSINESS

Intel Is Still Mostly a PC Chip Maker

Client Computing (chips for laptops and desktops) is the biggest piece of Intel at $32.2 billion, 61% of sales, but it shrank 3.4% last year. Intel Foundry, which makes chips for other companies, and Datacenter and AI, which sells server chips, are both smaller but growing -- up 2.9% and 4.9% respectively. The company is betting its future on those two growing pieces outrunning the shrinking core PC business.

FINANCIAL HEALTH

Growing Sales, Still Losing Money Overall

Revenue grew 20.9% recently, but Intel's overall profit margin is negative 20% -- it loses about 20 cents for every sales dollar after all costs. It does keep a 40% gross margin (40 cents of every sales dollar before overhead) and a positive 10.3% operating margin, but one-time costs and charges are dragging the bottom line negative. Intel holds $54 billion in cash against $50 billion in debt -- a healthy cushion -- but free cash flow is thin at just $806 million for a company this size.

Analysts Say 'Hold,' Wide Target Range

The 43 analysts covering Intel rate it a consensus 'hold' (third-party opinion), with price targets ranging from $63 to $160 and an average of about $90.65 -- well above the current price, but that average predates recent earnings shocks. HISTORY & ODDS

Deep Drawdowns Have Mixed Outcomes

In 6 past episodes where Intel traded this far below its highs and under its 200-day average, the stock was higher 3 months later in roughly two-thirds of cases (average +13.8%), but 12 months out it was a coin flip -- up half the time, with outcomes ranging from -33.8% to +105.7%. This is a small sample (6 cases), so it's suggestive, not a reliable guide.

THE BULL CASE
  • Datacenter and AI revenue grew 4.9% and Foundry grew 2.9% -- the two businesses Intel needs to carry its future are both expanding.
  • A $54 billion cash balance gives Intel real room to keep investing through the turnaround without an immediate funding crisis.
  • Past deep-drawdown setups like this one were followed by a higher stock 3 months later in 4 of 6 cases.
THE BEAR CASE
  • The core Client Computing business, still 61% of sales, shrank 3.4% last year with no clear sign of stabilizing.
  • Overall profit margin is negative 20%, and free cash flow of $806 million is thin relative to a company with $50 billion in debt.
  • Nvidia cutting its stake and shareholder lawsuits following Monday's 28.6% crash point to deteriorating confidence, not a one-day blip.
