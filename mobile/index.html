THIS MORNING · Premarket

Jobs Blowout Spooks the Fed; Chips Bounce Back Anyway

October payrolls came in nearly triple what was expected, and with the Fed in hiking mode, stronger jobs data argues for fewer cuts, not more -- the 10-year held near 4.90% and the dollar firmed on it. That hawkish read is colliding with a chip-sector relief rally: Technology is adding 0.879 points to the S&P on its own, enough to explain the index's entire 0.14% gain and then some, while Health Care and Staples keep sliding.

Top Story Blowout Jobs Report Complicates the Fed's Next Move The 10-year sits at 4.90% (-0.05 today per the KPI data) and the dollar index is up 0.63% -- but this print landed just 12 minutes before this pull, so that reaction is still forming, not settled.
Also Moving Chip Stocks Claw Back a Chunk of Thursday's Rout Why Reuters and Yahoo Finance report chip names are rebounding after Thursday's "bloodbath," with Lam Research up 11.77% on a newly approved $25 billion buyback (per ad-hoc-news.de and Barron's) and TSMC surging 7% after Taiwan restricted short selling, per Benzinga. Technology is 38.2% of the S&P and up 2.3% this morning, contributing 0.879 points -- more than six times the index's actual 0.14% move (share_of_sp500_move_pct: 628) -- Nvidia alone (+4.24%, weight 7.6%) adds 0.321 points of that.
Worth Knowing Healthcare and Staples Quietly Lead the Selling Why WSJ reports healthcare stocks are falling as "market rout spreads," and 24/7 Wall St. notes defensives like Healthcare and Staples are "holding the line" relative to Tech's swings -- but both sectors are still down on the day, with Stryker (-2.86%) and Veeva (-2.93%) among Health Care's biggest decliners. Health Care is the single biggest drag on the S&P at -0.155 points, more than offsetting Tech's rebound -- a sign the relief rally is narrower than the headline Nasdaq bounce suggests.
Why It’s Happening

Why did jobs data beat so big, and why are chip stocks bouncing at the same time?

The trigger
CNBC reports October nonfarm payrolls rose 165,000 versus roughly 59,000 expected, with unemployment steady at 4.1%. Separately, Reuters and multiple outlets report chip stocks are rebounding after Thursday's steep AI-driven selloff, helped by Lam Research's newly approved $25 billion buyback and Taiwan's move to restrict short selling on TSMC.
Why it hits these stocks
A much-stronger-than-expected jobs report, with the Fed already in a hiking posture (last move was a hike to 4.00% in September), raises the odds the Fed holds or hikes again rather than cutting -- that pressures longer-duration growth names through higher yields. At the same time, Thursday's chip selloff was a repricing of AI capital-spending worries; today's bounce reflects buyers stepping back in on concrete capital-return news (Lam's buyback) and a short-selling restriction in Taiwan, not a resolution of the underlying AI-spending debate.
Who’s saying what
  • CNBC October payrolls of 165,000 came in far above the roughly 59,000 forecast, with unemployment holding at 4.1%.
  • Reuters Nasdaq futures rose after the steep AI selloff as chip stocks stabilize, framing today's move as a bounce rather than a resolution.
  • Barron's Lam Research "picked a good time" to announce its buyback, tying the stock's bounce directly to the capital-return news.
  • Morningstar Asks directly whether Europe's AI-stock selloff is overdone, representing the view that the rout has run ahead of the fundamentals.
The other side
The bounce could be short-covering after an outsized drop rather than a real resolution -- Health Care, Staples and Financials are still falling, and the Nikkei is down nearly 6% overnight, suggesting the broader AI-spending anxiety hasn't actually cleared.
What settles it
Tuesday's CPI print (Nov 10) is the next real test -- a hot number on top of today's strong jobs data would harden the case for the Fed holding or hiking further, pressuring the same long-duration tech names now trying to recover.

Agilent sells the instruments, chemicals and software that labs use to test drugs, food and materials -- think of it as a toolmaker for scientists. The business is growing steadily, with $6.95 billion in revenue last year and roughly 20 cents of profit kept on every sales dollar. The stock has climbed 18.5% over the past year and sits just 5.3% below its 52-week high. The one thing that matters most right now: a Q4 earnings report lands November 23, the next test of whether this steady growth story keeps its momentum.

THE BUSINESS

Agilent Is a Lab-Equipment and Services Company

Its biggest line, Agilent CrossLab ($2.9 billion, 42% of sales), sells the repairs, parts, chemicals and software labs need to keep running day to day. Life Sciences and Diagnostics ($2.7 billion) sells the actual testing machines -- like chromatography systems that separate and measure chemicals -- and is growing fastest, up 10.5% last year. A smaller Applied Markets unit ($1.3 billion) serves industrial and chemical testing.

HISTORY & ODDS

Historically Higher a Year After Setups Like This
